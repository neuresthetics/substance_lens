# Philosophy vs. sophistry: a study for a working distinction

*Prepared for Jason Burns, 1 October 2026. Every direct quotation below was checked against a fetched text: the Jowett translations of Plato on Project Gutenberg, the Pickard-Cambridge and Rhys Roberts translations of Aristotle in the MIT Internet Classics Archive, Saunders' translation of Schopenhauer, the Elwes translation of Spinoza, the SEP and the IEP. Where I give only the sense of a passage, I say "paraphrase". The links and references at the end say which source I relied on for each claim.*

---

## 0. The short answer

Philosophy and sophistry use **the same tools**: definitions, distinctions, questions, examples, analogies, long speeches and short answers. Since the 1830s scholars have shown repeatedly that you cannot tell them apart by method alone. What separates them is **what the speaker is trying to get, and whether the speaker lets the moves be checked.**

- **Philosophy** offers every step for inspection. Its key words keep one meaning, its premises are stated where they can be challenged, the other side's view is put in a form its holder would accept, and the speaker is prepared to lose, because finding out matters more than winning.
- **Sophistry** produces the *appearance* of having shown something. Its moves work *only if nobody slows down*: words that quietly change meaning, premises nobody stated, playing to the audience rather than the interlocutor, and attacks on the person or on a convenient caricature. Its aim is victory, reputation or payment, not understanding.

The test anyone can use: **would this move still work if both sides slowed down, said exactly what their words meant, and asked at every step "what exactly follows from what?"** If the move survives that slowing-down, it is philosophy, even if it is wrong. If it only works at speed, it is sophistry, even if its conclusion happens to be true.

Aristotle drew the line in the same place. Sophistry and dialectic share a faculty and differ in "moral purpose" (*Rhetoric* I.1, 1355b), or in the "choice of life" (*Metaphysics* IV.2, 1004b). Spinoza's version: confused words and images pass themselves off as ideas, and the cure is order, i.e. definitions first, then each step shown (TIE §§88–90; *Ethics* II P47 schol., P49 schol.).

The rest of this document gives the history, the sources and the places where this line is genuinely disputed.

## 1. Who the Sophists were

"Sophist" (*sophistēs*) first meant simply a wise man or expert. Poets and sages could be called sophists. In the second half of the fifth century BCE the word came to mean something narrower: a travelling professional teacher who charged fees for instruction, above all in **speaking well and succeeding in public life** (IEP "Sophists", §1; SEP "The Sophists"). Democratic Athens rewarded persuasive speech in the assembly and the courts, so their teaching had obvious value. It also drew suspicion: it was new, it was sold for money, and it was open to anyone who could pay, unlike the traditional aristocratic upbringing (IEP §1).

Almost everything we know comes from **hostile witnesses**, chiefly Plato and Aristotle, plus fragments collected in Diels–Kranz (DK). We should therefore read Plato's portraits as arguments rather than reports.

| Figure | What he is known for | Key sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Protagoras** of Abdera (c. 490–420) | He was the first to call himself a sophist and to charge fees (Plato, *Protagoras* 349a, per IEP). He taught *aretē* in the sense of political skill. In Plato's dialogue he promises "prudence in affairs private as well as public" (*Prot.* 318e–319a, Jowett). He is famous for "Man is the measure of all things" (*Theaetetus* 152a) and for the claim that there are two opposed arguments on every matter (antilogic; Diogenes Laertius IX.51, paraphrase). Aristotle reports that he offered training in "making the worse argument seem the better" and calls this "a fraud" (*Rhet.* II.24, 1402a). | Plato, *Protagoras*, *Theaetetus*; DK 80 |
| **Gorgias** of Leontini (c. 485–c. 390) | A rhetorician who came to Athens as an ambassador in 427. His *Encomium of Helen* calls speech (*logos*) a powerful master that can drug the soul like medicine. That is IEP's rendering, and I give the sense, not a translation. | Plato, *Gorgias*; DK 82 B3, B11 |
| **Prodicus** of Ceos | Known for fine distinctions between near-synonyms, the "correctness of names". In Plato's *Protagoras* he tells the audience to listen to both speakers "impartially, but not equally" (337a, Cooper trans. via IEP). Socrates calls himself, perhaps ironically, a pupil of Prodicus (*Prot.* 341a; *Meno* 96d). | Plato, *Protagoras*, *Euthydemus* 277e; DK 84 |
| **Hippias** of Elis | A polymath: astronomy, mathematics, music, poetry, history and a trained memory (DK 86 A14, per IEP). He is associated with the contrast between nature and convention (*physis* vs *nomos*). | Plato, *Hippias Major*, *Hippias Minor*, *Protagoras* 337c–d |
| **Thrasymachus** of Chalcedon | A rhetorician known to us almost entirely through Plato. In *Republic* I he says that justice is "the interest of the stronger" (338c). | Plato, *Republic* I, *Cleitophon* |

Common themes in their thought were *physis* vs *nomos*, relativism about truth or value (Protagoras), the power of speech (Gorgias), precision about words (Prodicus), and education as a saleable skill (IEP §3).

## 2. Plato's portrait of the sophist

Plato did more than anyone to make "sophist" a term of abuse, but his portraits are subtle. They make five charges.

**(a) Persuasion without knowledge: *Gorgias*.** Socrates gets Gorgias to agree to "two sorts of persuasion,—one which is the source of belief without knowledge, as the other is of knowledge" (454e). Rhetoric produces the second kind. Socrates then calls rhetoric not an art but "an experience in producing a sort of delight and gratification" (462c), a form of **flattery** that stands to justice as cookery stands to medicine: it aims at what pleases, not at what is good (paraphrase of 464b–465d). The charge is not that rhetoricians lie. It is that their success does not depend on knowing whether what they say is true.

**(b) Long speeches against short answers: *Protagoras*.** The dialogue breaks down over method. Socrates asks Protagoras to answer briefly, Protagoras prefers long speeches, and the bystanders, Prodicus and Hippias among them, have to negotiate a procedure (paraphrase of 334c–338e). The quarrel matters because an argument can be inspected step by step only in short exchanges, while a long speech can carry the audience past weak steps. Plato is honest enough to show Socrates giving long speeches himself, and Protagoras' "great speech" (320c–328d) is serious political theory, not mere display.

**(c) Eristic, or argument as a game of tripping: *Euthydemus*.** The brothers Euthydemus and Dionysiodorus refute a boy whatever he answers. They do it by trading on the double meaning of "learn" (*manthanein*: "to acquire knowledge" and "to understand"). Socrates explains the trick. This is "the first part of the sophistical ritual, which, as Prodicus says, begins with initiation into the correct use of terms". Knowing such things gives no knowledge of reality; it only lets one "play with men, tripping them up and oversetting them with distinctions of words", like pulling a stool from under someone about to sit (277e–278b, Jowett). The dialogue is a museum of fallacies: equivocation, *secundum quid* and composition, many of them later classified by Aristotle. The brothers also argue, roughly at 285d–286b, that contradiction is impossible (paraphrase).

**(d) The definition of the sophist: *Sophist*.** The Eleatic Stranger tries to define the sophist by successive division and produces several portraits. He summarises them at 231d: the sophist is a "paid hunter after wealth and youth", a "merchant in the goods of the soul", a retailer and a manufacturer of such goods, and a "hero of debate, who professed the eristic art". Along the way he finds a "noble sophistry" that purifies souls by refutation (231b), which looks uncomfortably like Socrates himself. The final definition (268c–d) makes the sophist an imitator of *appearance*: "the conscious or dissembling section of the art of causing self-contradiction, is an imitator of appearance … the juggling of words, a creation human, and not divine". Saying what is *false* requires saying what is *not*, so pinning the sophist down forces Plato into the problem of not-being. The difficulty of the definition is itself part of Plato's point.

**(e) Might as right: *Republic* I.** Thrasymachus claims that justice is "the interest of the stronger" (338c). Socrates refutes him with a craft analogy: the ruler *qua* ruler, like the doctor *qua* doctor, serves the interest of those he cares for (paraphrase of 340c–342e). Many readers since have found Socrates' arguments here thin, even sophistical, and Plato seems to agree, because Books II–X start again from the beginning. Thrasymachus also accuses Socrates of only asking questions and never giving his own answer (paraphrase of 336c). That accusation is the first recorded complaint that Socratic questioning can be a debating tactic.

**What Plato's critique amounts to.** IEP's George Duke identifies two themes: the sophists' **mercenary** character and their **overestimation of the power of speech** (IEP §4). Xenophon has Socrates call sophists "prostitutes of wisdom" because they sell to anyone (*Memorabilia* I.6.13, per IEP). On this reading the sophist treats knowledge as a finished product to be sold, and speech as a tool for mastering people. The philosopher treats knowledge as something pursued, never finally possessed, and speech as answerable to how things are.

## 3. Aristotle: sophistical refutations, and rhetoric as a legitimate art

Aristotle gives the first systematic account, and it remains the deepest.

### 3.1 *On Sophistical Refutations* (*De Sophisticis Elenchis*)

- **Definition.** "The art of the sophist is the semblance of wisdom without the reality, and the sophist is one who makes money from an apparent but unreal wisdom" (ch. 1, 165a21). The book concerns "arguments which appear to be refutations but are really fallacies and not refutations" (164a20).
- **Four kinds of argument in dialogue** (ch. 2). *Didactic* arguments reason from the proper principles of a subject. *Dialectical* arguments reason from generally accepted premises. *Examination* (peirastic) arguments reason from what the answerer accepts. *Contentious* arguments "reason or appear to reason to a conclusion from premisses that appear to be generally accepted but are not so". The point is that **legitimate argument comes in several kinds**, and sophistry imitates the dialectical kind.
- **Five aims of the contentious arguer** (ch. 3): "refutation, fallacy, paradox, solecism, and fifthly to reduce the opponent … to babbling". These are aims of *managing an opponent*, not of finding something out.
- **The thirteen fallacies** (chs. 4–5). Six depend on language: "ambiguity, amphiboly, combination, division of words, accent, form of expression". Seven are outside language: accident, *secundum quid*, ignorance of what refutation is (*ignoratio elenchi*), begging the question, consequent, non-cause as cause, and many questions. All thirteen are rows in `fallacy_catalog.csv`.
- **Foul play** (ch. 11, c. 171b). This is the key passage on the distinction. "The art of contentious reasoning is foul fighting in disputation", like a foul in a race. Those who do it "in order to win the mere victory are generally considered to be contentious and quarrelsome persons, while those who do it to win a reputation with a view to making money are sophistical". Aristotle adds that "quarrelsome persons and sophists both employ the same arguments, but not with the same motives". Crucially, a merely apparent reasoning is contentious "even though its conclusion be true". **Sophistry does not require a false conclusion.**
- **The first treatment of the subject.** Aristotle claims no earlier treatment of the subject existed (ch. 34, 183b34).

### 3.2 *Rhetoric*

Aristotle refuses Plato's wholesale condemnation of rhetoric. Rhetoric is "the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion" (I.2). It is the counterpart of dialectic. It is useful because truth does not always win unaided, and we should be able to argue both sides "in order that we may see clearly what the facts are" (I.1, paraphrase). Then comes the decisive sentence for our purposes:

> "What makes a man a 'sophist' is not his faculty, but his moral purpose … a man is a 'sophist' because he has a certain kind of moral purpose, a 'dialectician' in respect, not of his moral purpose, but of his faculty." (*Rhet.* I.1, 1355b)

In other words, a sophist is defined by **what he is trying to do**, not by any special technique. Aristotle also catalogues nine "lines of spurious enthymeme" (*Rhet.* II.24, 1400b34–1402a28). These are rhetorical counterparts of the sophistical refutations, ending with Protagoras' "making the worse argument seem the better".

### 3.3 *Metaphysics* IV.2

> "Sophistic is Wisdom which exists only in semblance … [philosophy] differs from dialectic in the nature of the faculty required and from sophistic in respect of the purpose of the philosophic life. Dialectic is merely critical where philosophy claims to know, and sophistic is what appears to be philosophy but is not." (1004b17–26)

This gives us a three-way map:

| | Same subject matter? | Differs from philosophy by… |
|---|---|---|
| **Dialectic** | yes | *capacity*: it tests and criticises; it does not claim knowledge |
| **Sophistic** | yes | *purpose / choice of life*: it seeks the appearance of wisdom |
| **Philosophy** | — | it seeks to know |

## 4. Schopenhauer: the honest manual of dishonesty

Schopenhauer's *Eristische Dialektik* was written around 1830–31 and published posthumously. The English translation used here, *The Art of Controversy*, is by T. B. Saunders (1896). The work is now often titled *The Art of Being Right*. Schopenhauer turns Aristotle's catalogue into a **practical manual**:

> "Controversial Dialectic is the art of disputing, and of disputing in such a way as to hold one's own, whether one is in the right or the wrong—*per fas et nefas*."

He grounds it in psychology rather than logic: "the natural baseness of human nature". If we were honourable we would care only about truth, but our "innate vanity … will not suffer us to allow that our first position was wrong". He then lists **38 stratagems**. Examples: extending the opponent's claim beyond its limits (1); exploiting homonymy (2); hiding the conclusion until the premises are granted; "That's all very well in theory, but it won't do in practice" (33); bombast (36); refuting a bad proof and claiming to have refuted the thesis (37); and finally the *argumentum ad personam*, becoming personal and insulting once you are losing, which he distinguishes from the legitimate *argumentum ad hominem* that argues from the opponent's own admissions (38).

Two passages matter for a lens project:

1. **Naming as defence.** "It would be a very good thing if every trick could receive some short and obviously appropriate name, so that when a man used this or that particular trick, he could be at once reproached for it." That is the case for a fallacy catalogue in one sentence.
2. **Choosing whom to argue with.** Schopenhauer ends by citing the last chapter of Aristotle's *Topics*: dispute only with those who "possess sufficient intelligence and self-respect not to advance absurdities; to appeal to reason and not to authority, and to listen to reason and yield to it; and, finally, to cherish truth, to be willing to accept reason even from an opponent, and to be just enough to bear being proved to be in the wrong". **That list is a description of a philosophical interlocutor.** Notice that it is a list of dispositions, not techniques.

Schopenhauer's lesson for the distinction is that sophistry is **a natural default** driven by vanity, not an exotic vice. All 38 stratagems are in the catalogue, tagged SCH 1–38. Many are filed as *rhetorical stratagems* because they are moves in managing a debate, not defective arguments.

## 5. Spinoza: words, imagination and the geometric order

Spinoza never wrote about "sophists" as such, but his theory of error is close to a theory of sophistry, and it fits a "lens" project exactly.

- **Words belong to the imagination, not the intellect.** "Words are a part of the imagination … words may, equally with the imagination, be the cause of many and great errors" (TIE §88). Words "are signs of things as existing in the imagination, not as existing in the understanding … Many things we affirm and deny, because the nature of words allows us to do so, though the nature of things does not" (TIE §89). That last sentence is close to a definition of verbal sophistry: **conclusions licensed by grammar rather than by things.** It covers Aristotle's "form of expression" and Mill's "referential fallacy" (wherever there is a name there is a thing).
- **Imagining is not understanding.** "We think that what we more readily imagine is clearer to us; and also we think that what we imagine we understand" (TIE §90). This is the mechanism behind misleading vividness, anecdote and appeals to emotion: what is easy to picture passes for what is true.
- **Most disputes are verbal.** "Many errors, in truth, can be traced to this head, namely, that we do not apply names to things rightly … Very many controversies have arisen from the fact, that men do not rightly explain their meaning, or do not rightly interpret the meaning of others" (*Ethics* II P47 schol.).
- **Ideas are not words or pictures.** Errors arise from confusing "images, words, and ideas". Spinoza writes of "those who confuse words with ideas" and holds that "an idea (being a mode of thinking) does not consist in the image of anything, nor in words. The essence of words and images is put together by bodily motions, which in no wise involve the conception of thought" (*Ethics* II P49 schol.).
- **General terms come from confused images.** Transcendental terms ("Being", "Thing") and universals ("man", "horse") arise when the body is overloaded with images and the mind blurs their differences (*Ethics* II P40 schol. 1). This explains why arguments built on abstract nouns are fertile ground for equivocation.
- **The cure is definitions and order.** A good definition must "explain the inmost essence of a thing, and must take care not to substitute for this any of its properties", must include the proximate cause (his example is the circle generated by a rotating line), and must be such that all of the thing's properties can be deduced from it (TIE §§95–96). The *Ethics* is "demonstrated in geometrical order": definitions, axioms, propositions, demonstrations and scholia. In the preface to Part III Spinoza promises to treat human emotions "as though I were concerned with lines, planes, and solids". **The geometric order works as an anti-sophistry device.** Every term is fixed before use, every step cites what it depends on, and nothing can be carried past a weak link by eloquence.
- **Truth corrects error by being understood, not by out-shouting it.** "Even as light displays both itself and darkness, so is truth a standard both of itself and of falsity" (*Ethics* II P43 schol.). On this view sophistry is not defeated by better sophistry but by an adequate idea of the matter, once someone actually understands it.
- **Ignorance dressed as explanation.** In the Appendix to *Ethics* I, Spinoza describes people who, pressed from cause to cause, "take refuge in the will of God—in other words, the sanctuary of ignorance". This is the pattern of the *ad hoc* rescue and the god-of-the-gaps argument.

**Spinoza's contribution to the distinction** is a mechanism. Sophistry works in the medium of imagination and words, while philosophy is answerable to the intellect's order of causes. The question to ask of a claim is: *is this following from the nature of the thing, or only from the nature of the words?*

## 6. The modern rehabilitation of the Sophists

From the nineteenth century onward, Plato's verdict has been challenged repeatedly.

- **Hegel** treated the sophists as a necessary stage in which thought turns subjective and reflective. They were sceptical subjectivists whose critique of earlier dogmatism was later synthesised by Plato and Aristotle (IEP §1, citing the *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*).
- **George Grote**, in the chapter on the sophists in his *History of Greece*, argued that "sophist" did not name a school or a doctrine. On his account Plato had turned a profession into a caricature, and the sophists were **progressive thinkers** who questioned the conventional morality of their day. IEP cites the 1904 edition, vol. 7; volume and chapter numbering differ between editions.
- **Henry Sidgwick**, "The Sophists", *Journal of Philology* 4 (1872), drew the methodological contrast: Socratic question-and-answer vs. sophistic display speeches. IEP notes that this cannot hold, because Gorgias and Protagoras claimed skill in short answers and Socrates gives long, mythic speeches.
- **Mario Untersteiner**, *The Sophists* (Eng. trans. K. Freeman, Blackwell 1954), offers a sympathetic philosophical reconstruction.
- **W. K. C. Guthrie**, *A History of Greek Philosophy* vol. III (Cambridge UP, 1969), parts of which were reissued as *The Sophists* (1971), treats them as serious thinkers about *physis* and *nomos*, relativism and language.
- **G. B. Kerferd**, *The Sophistic Movement* (Cambridge UP, 1981), distinguishes three things. **Eristic** seeks victory regardless of truth. **Antilogic** opposes one argument to another, forcing the opponent to give up a position or hold both. **Dialectic** is Plato's method aimed at truth. Kerferd argues that the sophists practised antilogic, a legitimate and important technique, and that Socrates himself used it as a step toward dialectic.
- **Alexander Nehamas**, "Eristic, Antilogic, Sophistic, Dialectic: Plato's Demarcation of Philosophy from Sophistry", *History of Philosophy Quarterly* 7 (1990): 3–16, argues that "Socrates did not differ from the sophists in method but in overall purpose" (p. 13, quoted by IEP). He also argues that Socratic *elenchus* cannot be told apart from antilogic by its form, because both succeed or fail according to whether the interlocutor can defend himself.
- **Susan Jarratt**, *Rereading the Sophists: Classical Rhetoric Refigured* (Southern Illinois UP, 1991), reclaims the sophists for rhetoric and composition studies. She presents them as teachers of situated, democratic and probabilistic reasoning, and as a resource for feminist critique of a philosophical tradition that defined itself against them. This is my characterisation of the book's general thrust, not a quotation.
- **Derrida and Lyotard.** Derrida's *Dissemination* (1981 trans.) reads Plato's trial of the sophists as foundational to Western philosophy's self-image: philosophy needed sophistry as its other. Lyotard (*Just Gaming*, with Thébaud, 1985) credits the sophists with seeing that talk of justice cannot leave the realm of opinion and language games (IEP §4).
- **SEP**, "The Sophists", by C. C. W. Taylor and Mi-Kyoung Lee, is the best current overview.

**What survives the rehabilitation.** IEP's conclusion is that the methodological line fails but the line drawn by *ethical orientation* survives. The sophist, as Plato and Aristotle portray him, chooses a way of life in which knowledge is a product and speech is power, while the philosopher pursues knowledge as a good in itself and stays aware that the pursuit is provisional. Pierre Hadot puts it this way (*What Is Ancient Philosophy?*, 2004, p. 174, quoted by IEP): people "who developed an apparently philosophical discourse without trying to live their lives in accordance with their discourse … were called sophists."

## 7. The modern informal-logic view: from "the sophist" to "the move"

Modern argumentation theory mostly stops asking *who* is a sophist and asks instead *which moves are illegitimate, and in what kind of dialogue*. This turns a distinction about character into one that can be checked.

- **C. L. Hamblin, *Fallacies* (London: Methuen, 1970).** Hamblin attacked the textbook "standard treatment" of fallacies as "as debased, worn-out and dogmatic a treatment as could be imagined—incredibly tradition bound, yet lacking in logic and in historical sense alike" (p. 12, quoted in SEP). He proposed **formal dialectic** instead: model arguments as rule-governed dialogues in which each participant has commitment stores, and define fallacies as violations of the dialogue's rules. Almost all later work descends from this.
- **John Woods and Douglas Walton**, in a long series of papers collected as *Fallacies: Selected Papers 1972–1982* (Foris, 1989), analysed individual fallacies one by one with whatever formal tools fitted. Their approach is often called methodological pluralism.
- **Pragma-dialectics** (Frans van Eemeren and Rob Grootendorst, Amsterdam; *Speech Acts in Argumentative Discussions* 1984, *Argumentation, Communication, and Fallacies* 1992, *A Systematic Theory of Argumentation* 2004). The ideal is a **critical discussion** aimed at resolving a difference of opinion *on the merits*, governed by ten rules. In the order given in van Eemeren 2018 they are: freedom, burden of proof, standpoint, relevance, unexpressed premise, starting point, argument scheme, validity, closure, and language use. A fallacy is "every violation of any of the rules" (2004, p. 175, quoted in SEP). This framework produces fallacies the tradition had no name for, such as *declaring a standpoint sacrosanct* and *evading the burden of proof*. Later work on **strategic manoeuvring** (van Eemeren 2010) accepts that arguers legitimately try to win, and treats fallacies as manoeuvring that "derails". The line is crossed when the pursuit of effectiveness overrides reasonableness.
- **Douglas Walton: argumentation schemes and critical questions** (*A Pragmatic Theory of Fallacy*, 1995; Walton, Reed & Macagno, *Argumentation Schemes*, Cambridge UP, 2008). Most everyday arguments are **defeasible schemes**, such as argument from expert opinion, from analogy, from consequences or from sign. Each scheme comes with **critical questions**. For expert opinion there are six: expertise, field, opinion, trustworthiness, consistency, backup evidence. An argument that answers its critical questions is reasonable. One that is pressed while blocking them is fallacious. Walton also distinguishes the **paralogism**, "the type of fallacy in which an error of reasoning is typically committed by failing to meet some necessary requirement of an argumentation scheme", from the **sophism**, "a sophistical tactic used to try to unfairly get the best of a speech partner in an exchange of arguments" (2010, p. 171, quoted in SEP). That is a modern restatement of the old distinction. He also stresses that what counts as fallacious depends on the **type of dialogue**: persuasion, inquiry, negotiation, deliberation, information-seeking or eristic. A threat is a fallacy in a critical discussion but a normal move in a negotiation.

**What this gives the distinction.** Informal logic replaces "the sophist has bad motives" with "**this move violates a rule that both parties would need to accept for the discussion to settle the question on its merits**". That can be checked without reading minds. It also gives the old idea, that philosophy is willing to be checked and sophistry is not, a precise form. Look at the pragma-dialectical rules: you may not block standpoints, dodge the burden of proof, distort the other's position, deny your own unstated premises, refuse agreed starting points, misuse argument schemes, refuse to accept defeat, or exploit unclear language. **Every one of them is a rule about letting the argument be checked.**

## 8. A working distinction anyone can apply

**In one sentence.** Philosophy is argument that *invites* checking and would accept defeat; sophistry is argument that *depends on not being checked* and aims at winning.

**Five plain questions.** None of them needs special vocabulary.

1. **Do the key words keep the same meaning throughout?** If "free", "natural", "violence" or "God" means one thing in the premise and another in the conclusion, the argument is trading on the word, not the thing (Euthydemus; Aristotle's six verbal fallacies; Spinoza TIE §89).
2. **Can every step be seen, including the unstated ones?** If the argument needs an assumption the speaker won't say out loud or won't own when asked, it is hiding something (pragma-dialectics, unexpressed-premise rule; Schopenhauer's concealment stratagems).
3. **Is the other side stated in a form they would accept?** If the opponent's view has to be exaggerated, cut off from its context, or replaced by its worst defender, that is a straw man, a quotation out of context, or nut-picking (Schopenhauer 1; *Rhet.* II.24).
4. **What would change the speaker's mind, and does the speaker accept the burden of proving the claim?** If no possible evidence counts, or the burden keeps sliding to the other side, the speaker is protecting a position, not testing it (unfalsifiability; burden-of-proof rule; *declaring a standpoint sacrosanct*).
5. **Does the move work better on an audience than on a careful reader?** Speed, crowd emotion, ridicule, intimidation and an avalanche of points are sophistry's natural medium. Slowing down is philosophy's (Plato's dispute over short answers and long speeches; Schopenhauer 36 on bombast; the Gish gallop).

A "no" to any of the first three questions, or a bad answer to the last two, is a *flag*, not a verdict. The questions point at where to look.

**What sophistry is *not*:**
- It is not the same as **being wrong**. Philosophers make honest mistakes all the time, and Walton would call these paralogisms.
- It is not the same as **reaching a false conclusion**. Aristotle is explicit that a contentious argument may have a true conclusion (*SE* 11).
- It is not the same as **rhetoric**. Aristotle defends rhetoric as an art, and good arguments may be well dressed.
- It is not the same as **being paid**, despite Plato. The modern professor and the modern lawyer are paid. The question is whether pay, or audience approval, *decides what gets argued*.
- It is not the same as **using a pattern that appears on a fallacy list**. Appeals to authority, slippery-slope arguments and arguments about a person's reliability are often legitimate (see `what_is_a_fallacy.md`).

## 9. Where the line is contested

1. **The Sophists were serious thinkers.** Protagorean relativism, Gorgias' scepticism about communication, and the nature/convention debate are philosophy by any measure (Grote, Guthrie, Kerferd). Using "sophist" as a slur risks repeating Plato's propaganda.
2. **Socrates argues fallaciously too.** Many arguments in the early dialogues are invalid, including some in *Republic* I against Thrasymachus. Plato himself describes Socratic refutation as a "noble sophistry" (*Sophist* 231b, per IEP). If method cannot separate them, we are left with purpose, which is hard to observe.
3. **Purpose is invisible.** Aristotle's "moral purpose" criterion is right in principle but cannot be checked from outside. The modern move, judging the *move* against the rules of the dialogue, is a workaround: it judges conduct rather than motive. The cost is that an honest arguer can commit a sophistical move by accident.
4. **Antilogic, or arguing both sides, is a legitimate exercise.** Arguing both sides is a teaching method, a legal necessity and a tool for testing ideas (Kerferd; Aristotle *Rhet.* I.1). It becomes eristic only when it is offered as a verdict.
5. **Is rhetoric legitimate?** Plato's *Gorgias* says no and Aristotle's *Rhetoric* says yes. Rhetoric scholars such as Jarratt argue that the "philosophy vs. sophistry" binary was itself a rhetorical move by philosophers to claim authority.
6. **The postmodern challenge.** Derrida and Lyotard argue that the line is drawn *by* philosophy in its own interest, and that discourse about justice never escapes opinion. Taken seriously, this makes the distinction a matter of politics, not logic. Most argumentation theorists answer that the dialogue rules are justified by the very goal of settling a disagreement on the merits, so anyone who argues at all is committed to them.
7. **The Fallacy Fork.** Boudry, Paglieri and Pigliucci (2015) argue that named fallacies are either precise but rare (deductive forms) or common but not always fallacious (context-dependent). If so, a checklist of named fallacies cannot by itself separate philosophy from sophistry. You need judgment about context, which is exactly what the five questions above ask for.
8. **Spinoza's rationalism.** Spinoza's confidence that the intellect can see the nature of things through adequate definitions is itself disputed. An empiricist or pragmatist will say that definitions are tools, not essences. The *practical* rule, fix your terms and show your steps, survives without the metaphysics.

---

## 10. Implications for a Spinoza-inspired lens

- **Detect the move, not the motive.** Flag violations of keep-terms-fixed, show-all-steps, state-the-other-side-fairly, accept-the-burden and allow-defeat. These can be checked against the text. Motive can be noted only as a hypothesis.
- **Separate three layers.** (i) Invalid *form*: formal fallacies, which can be checked mechanically. (ii) Defeasible schemes used without answering their *critical questions*: most informal fallacies, which need context. (iii) *Conduct* of the exchange: stratagems and rule violations, which need the dialogue history. Biases are a fourth thing: *causes* of error, not errors in arguments.
- **Make the Spinozan test explicit.** For every key term, ask: *was it defined, and does it keep that definition?* For every inference, ask: *does this follow from the nature of the thing, or only from the nature of the words?* (TIE §89).
- **Guard against the fallacy fallacy.** Showing that an argument is bad does not show that its conclusion is false (Schopenhauer 37), and the lens should say so in its own output.
