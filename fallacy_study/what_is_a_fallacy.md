# What is a fallacy?

*Prepared for Jason Burns, 1 October 2026. Quotations were checked against fetched texts. Most definitions by modern authors are quoted as they appear in Hans Hansen's SEP article "Fallacies" (rev. 2024) or in Bradley Dowden's IEP article "Fallacies"; the original books were not consulted directly. Paraphrases are marked.*

---

## 1. The short answer

A fallacy is **a bad move in reasoning or argument that is liable to be mistaken for a good one**. There are three things in that definition.

1. **It is a move, not just a belief.** Calling "the popular belief that we use 10% of our brains" a fallacy is common, but scholars mostly reserve the word for *arguments* or *moves in argument*. SEP calls these the "belief" and "argument" conceptions of fallacy, and notes that academic writers prefer the argument conception.
2. **It is bad in a particular way.** Its premises fail to support its conclusion in the way they claim to, or the move breaks a rule of fair discussion. A false premise alone does not make a fallacy, and neither does a false conclusion.
3. **It looks good.** The "semblance" condition is what separates a fallacy from a mere blunder. As IEP puts it, "A fallacy is a mistake, but not every mistake is a fallacy."

Researchers do not agree on the details. Dowden (IEP) notes that they are "deeply divided, not only about how to define the term 'fallacy' and how to define some of the individual fallacies, but also about whether there are necessary and sufficient conditions for distinguishing between fallacious and non-fallacious reasoning generally."

## 2. Definitions through history

| Source | Definition | What it emphasises |
|---|---|---|
| **Aristotle**, *On Sophistical Refutations* 1, 164a20 | "arguments which appear to be refutations but are really fallacies and not refutations". The sophist's art is "the semblance of wisdom without the reality" (165a21). | **Appearance vs. reality** in a *dialogue* setting: a refutation is a move against an opponent's thesis. |
| **Locke**, *Essay* IV.xvii.19–22 (1690) | Not a definition of "fallacy". Locke names four "sorts of arguments" men use to get others to assent: *ad verecundiam* (appeal to modesty or respect for authority), *ad ignorantiam*, *ad hominem* ("to press a man with consequences drawn from his own principles or concessions") and *ad judicium* (from proofs). Only the last gives real knowledge. Locke did **not** call the first three fallacies (SEP §2.4). | It shows that the "ad" arguments began as *legitimate but limited* dialectical moves. |
| **Bentham**, *The Book of Fallacies* (1824) | He sorts political fallacies by their *purpose* into fallacies of authority, danger, delay and confusion. Their function is to block or delay discussion of a measure on its merits (paraphrase). | Fallacies as **obstruction of deliberation**, an early dialectical view. |
| **Whately**, *Elements of Logic* (1826) Bk III | "any unsound mode of arguing, which appears to demand our conviction, and to be decisive of the question at hand, when in fairness it is not" (quoted in SEP). | Unsoundness plus the appearance of being decisive. |
| **Mill**, *System of Logic* (1843) Bk V | "A catalogue of the varieties of evidence which are not real evidence … is an enumeration of fallacies" (V.iii §1, quoted in SEP). Mill separates intellectual errors from the "moral" causes, such as bias and interest, that make us accept them. | Fallacy as **apparent evidence**, and an early separation of fallacy from bias. |
| **Copi**, *Introduction to Logic* (1961, p. 52) | "a form of argument that seems to be correct but which proves, upon examination, not to be so" (quoted in SEP). | The textbook formula. |
| **The "standard definition"** (Hamblin 1970, p. 12) | An argument "that seems to be valid but is not so". SEP calls this SDF: (i) an argument, (ii) invalid, (iii) appears valid. | Hamblin attacked the *standard treatment* built around this definition. SEP notes, citing Hansen 2002, that the definition was never as universal as Hamblin implied. |
| **Walton**, *A Pragmatic Theory of Fallacy* (1995, p. 255) | Five parts: something that (1) is an argument or purports to be one, (2) falls short of some standard of correctness, (3) is used in a context of dialogue, (4) has a semblance of correctness, and (5) poses a serious problem to the realisation of the goal of the dialogue. The short form: "a deceptively bad argument that impedes the progress of a dialogue" (p. 256). | **Pragmatic**: the same argument can be fallacious in one dialogue and fine in another. |
| **Pragma-dialectics** (van Eemeren & Grootendorst 2004, p. 175) | "every violation of any of the rules of the discussion procedure for conducting a critical discussion" (quoted in SEP). | **Procedural**: no appearance condition is needed, and any rule violation counts. New fallacies follow, e.g. *declaring a standpoint sacrosanct*. |
| **Finocchiaro** (1981, 1987) | He doubts that "fallacy" is a real logical category. He prefers to speak of fallacious *arguments*, drops the appearance condition, and distinguishes six kinds of fallaciousness: formal, explanatory, presuppositional, positive, semantical and persuasive (SEP §3.2). | Real arguments are rarely textbook fallacies. |
| **Hahn & Oaksford** (2006, 2007) | Bayesian: an argument's strength depends on prior belief, the evidence and rival hypotheses. A fallacy is an argument that is *weak* on this measure, so different tokens of the same type can be strong or weak (SEP §3.3). | **Epistemic or probabilistic**: what matters is content, not form or procedure. |

The history moves from **appearance** (Aristotle, Whately, Copi, SDF), to **dialogue and procedure** (Hamblin, Walton, pragma-dialectics), to **strength of evidence** (Hahn & Oaksford). A good lens can use all three, as long as it says which one it is applying.

## 3. Hamblin and the "standard treatment"

C. L. Hamblin's *Fallacies* (Methuen, 1970) is the founding text of modern fallacy theory. He surveyed textbook chapters on fallacies and found them

> "as debased, worn-out and dogmatic a treatment as could be imagined—incredibly tradition bound, yet lacking in logic and in historical sense alike, and almost without connection to anything else in modern Logic at all." (p. 12, quoted in SEP)

He made three complaints, given here as paraphrase:

- **The definition doesn't fit the list.** Many traditional fallacies are not invalid arguments at all. *Petitio principii* is formally valid ("P, therefore P"). Many questions is not an argument. *Ad hominem* is often an inductive, defeasible argument whose quality is a matter of degree.
- **The examples are artificial.** Textbook examples are invented and too obvious to deceive anyone. Finocchiaro later pressed this point.
- **No theory.** The list was handed down as a list, with no account of *why* these moves are wrong.

Hamblin's remedy was **formal dialectic**: model argument as a rule-governed exchange between parties with commitment stores, and define fallacies as rule violations. Woods & Walton, pragma-dialectics and Walton's pragmatic theory all grow from that remedy.

## 4. Formal vs. informal fallacies

| | **Formal fallacy** | **Informal fallacy** |
|---|---|---|
| Where the fault lies | In the **logical form** alone. Any argument with that form, whatever its content, fails to be valid. | In the **content, context or use**: an ambiguity, an irrelevant premise, a missing presumption, an unanswered critical question, or an unfair procedure. |
| Examples | Affirming the consequent; denying the antecedent; undistributed middle; illicit major/minor; quantifier shift; existential fallacy; base-rate neglect, conjunction fallacy and prosecutor's fallacy as *probabilistic* formal errors. | Equivocation, straw man, *ad hominem*, slippery slope, appeal to authority, begging the question, hasty generalization, post hoc. |
| How you show it | Give a counter-example of the **same form** with true premises and a false conclusion. | Show the **specific defect**: the shift of meaning, the irrelevance, or the unanswered question. |
| Always fallacious? | The *form* is always invalid, but a particular argument may also have another, valid form (Massey's asymmetry, below). Arguments of an invalid form can still be good *inductive* or *abductive* arguments, so affirming the consequent is the skeleton of inference to the best explanation. | **No.** Most have legitimate instances (section 7). |

**Massey's asymmetry thesis** (1981, via SEP) adds a complication. You can prove an argument valid by showing it has a valid form. You cannot prove it invalid just by showing it has an invalid form, because every argument instantiates *some* invalid form (e.g. "P, Q, therefore R"). So even formal fallacy charges depend on interpretation: you must show that the argument has *no* valid reading that the arguer could reasonably have meant.

The catalogue uses the families *formal: propositional / syllogistic / quantificational-modal / probabilistic* and *informal: relevance / ambiguity / presumption / insufficient evidence / causal / statistical / dialectical-procedural*. These are conventions. Many fallacies fit more than one family, and the textbooks disagree about where to put them.

## 5. A fallacy vs. a weak or false argument

These four are often confused, and only the last is a fallacy:

1. **A false conclusion.** A valid argument from a false premise can reach a false conclusion without any fallacy. "All birds fly; penguins are birds; so penguins fly" is valid, not fallacious: the fault is a false premise.
2. **A false premise.** This is a factual error, to be corrected by evidence, not by naming a fallacy.
3. **A weak argument.** An argument that gives *some* support but not enough, such as a small sample or a modest correlation, is weak. It becomes a fallacy (*hasty generalization*, *cum hoc*) when the support is presented or taken as decisive.
4. **A fallacy.** A characteristic *kind* of error, usually recurring and named, that **looks better than it is** (appearance condition) or **breaks a rule of fair discussion** (procedural condition).

Three more distinctions:

- **Fallacy vs. bias.** Confirmation bias, anchoring and the availability heuristic are *psychological tendencies*, i.e. causes of error, not errors in arguments. They explain *why* fallacies tempt us. Mill already separated fallacies from their "moral" causes, and SEP §4.4 reviews the literature linking them (Correia, Thagard and others). The catalogue lists such items separately under "cognitive bias (not a fallacy)".
- **Fallacy vs. stratagem.** Some named "fallacies" are not defective *arguments* but tricks in *conducting* a debate: provoking anger, rushing, a flood of points, or diversion. Walton distinguishes **paralogisms** ("the type of fallacy in which an error of reasoning is typically committed by failing to meet some necessary requirement of an argumentation scheme") from **sophisms** ("a sophistical tactic used to try to unfairly get the best of a speech partner in an exchange of arguments") (2010, p. 171, quoted in SEP). Schopenhauer's 38 stratagems are mostly sophisms in this sense.
- **The fallacy fallacy.** Showing that an argument is fallacious does **not** show that its conclusion is false. Inferring falsity from a bad proof is itself a named error: the *argument from fallacy*, which Schopenhauer turns into a deliberate trick in stratagem 37.

## 6. The Fallacy Fork (Boudry, Paglieri & Pigliucci 2015)

Maarten Boudry, Fabio Paglieri and Massimo Pigliucci, "The Fake, the Flimsy, and the Fallacious: Demarcating Arguments in Real Life", *Argumentation* 29(4) (2015): 431–456, doi:10.1007/s10503-015-9359-1.

**The argument, paraphrased.** Take any named informal fallacy and try to define it. You face a fork:

- **Horn 1: a strict, formal definition.** You can define it as a deductively invalid pattern, e.g. "X preceded Y, *therefore necessarily* X caused Y". The definition is clear, but almost nobody argues that way in real life. These are **fake** fallacies, strawmen of real reasoning.
- **Horn 2: a loose, contextual definition.** You can define it as a defeasible argument that goes wrong in certain contexts, e.g. "inferring causation from sequence *when the evidence doesn't warrant it*". Now you find real examples, but the label has lost its **normative bite**. Whether a case is fallacious depends on the background evidence and the context, not on the pattern, so you have to argue the case on its merits anyway. Such arguments are often merely **flimsy** (weak) rather than fallacious, or perfectly good.

Their cases include post hoc, *ad hominem*, *ad ignorantiam* and the genetic fallacy. They also discuss pragma-dialectics. Their conclusion is that fallacy theory should give way to the study of the *conditions under which* defeasible arguments are strong or weak, which is close to Walton's critical questions and to Hahn & Oaksford's Bayesian view.

**Implications for an engine:**
- A detector that fires on surface patterns such as "expert", "if we allow X" or "he's a hypocrite" will mostly produce **false positives**.
- Every flag should come with a critical question that the argument fails to answer, rather than just a label.
- The catalogue's `not_fallacious_when` column exists for this reason.

## 7. Why many "fallacies" are often legitimate reasoning

| Pattern | Legitimate when… | Fallacious when… | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Appeal to authority** | The authority is a real expert *in the relevant field*, is unbiased and consistent with other experts, actually said this, and the claim is the kind experts can settle. These are Walton's six critical questions: expertise, field, opinion, trustworthiness, consistency, backup evidence. Bentham gave similar conditions for reliable authority in 1824. | The authority is outside its field, is anonymous, is contradicted by consensus, or is used to *end* the discussion rather than inform it. | IEP quotes Schwartz: "Some appeals to authority are fallacious; most are not." Walton 1997, *Appeal to Expert Opinion*. |
| **Slippery slope** | There is a real mechanism by which step A makes step B more likely, such as precedent, shifted norms, lowered costs or political dynamics, and the probabilities are argued for. | The chain is asserted without mechanism, or each "small step" is unlikely and the probabilities are multiplied as if they were certainties. | Walton, *Slippery Slope Arguments* (1992); Volokh, "The Mechanisms of the Slippery Slope", *Harvard Law Review* 116 (2003); Hahn & Oaksford. |
| ***Ad hominem*** | The person's reliability *is* the question: testimony, eyewitness credibility, conflicts of interest in a recommendation. Or the argument is *ex concessis*, showing that the opponent's own commitments conflict (Locke's original sense; Schopenhauer 16). | The person's character or circumstances are used to dismiss an *argument* whose validity does not depend on them. | Locke IV.xvii.21; Walton, *Ad Hominem Arguments* (1998); SEP §2.4. |
| **Argument from ignorance** | We searched where evidence would be expected and found none ("no smoke detected, so probably no fire"), or a legal or dialectical presumption assigns the burden, as with the presumption of innocence. | Lack of disproof is treated as proof, or the burden is shifted to the side that does not bear it. | Walton, *Arguments from Ignorance* (1996); Boudry et al. 2015. |
| **Post hoc / correlation** | Sequence and correlation are *evidence* of causation, and they are how causal hypotheses begin. They become strong with controls, a known mechanism, a dose-response relation and replication. | Sequence or correlation alone is treated as *proof*, with confounders, chance and reverse causation ignored. | Boudry et al. 2015; Mill's methods (*Logic* III.viii). |
| **Genetic argument** | The origin of a belief bears on its *reliability*: a rumour from a known liar, or a belief caused by a process that does not track truth. | The origin is used to settle the *truth* of a claim that has independent evidence. | Boudry et al. 2015. |
| **Appeal to consequences** | In *practical* reasoning (should we do X?), consequences are exactly what matters. | The question is factual (is X true?), and unwelcome consequences are offered as evidence that X is false. | Walton's argument from consequences scheme. |
| **Appeal to popularity** | The fact depends on convention (which side of the road people drive on), or many independent, informed judgements aggregate well (the "wisdom of crowds"). | Popularity is offered as proof of a fact that does not depend on opinion. | IEP; Hahn & Oaksford. |
| **Tu quoque** | It challenges the *consistency* or sincerity of the person advising, which is relevant when that is the issue. | It is used to deny the truth of the advice. | IEP; Schopenhauer 16. |
| **Circularity** | Some circles are benign or unavoidable. Definitions within a coherent system, the justification of basic rules of inference, and "virtuous" coherence are debated cases. | The conclusion is assumed in a premise *the other side doesn't accept*, so the argument cannot persuade anyone not already persuaded. | SEP §1 (begging the question); Walton. |
| **Composition** | Some properties do transfer from parts to wholes: a wall made entirely of red bricks is red. | The property is non-additive or relational: good players do not guarantee a good team. | SEP §1. |
| **Appeal to emotion or fear** | The emotion fits the facts: fear of a real danger, or compassion that rightly bears on a decision about how to treat people. | The emotion replaces the evidence, or is out of proportion to it. | Walton, *The Place of Emotion in Argument* (1992). |

The general lesson is that **most informal fallacies are corrupted versions of legitimate defeasible argument schemes.** The fallacy lies not in using the scheme but in (a) presenting a defeasible conclusion as conclusive, (b) ignoring the critical questions that would defeat it in this case, or (c) using it to block a discussion rather than advance it.

## 8. A working definition for the lens

> **A move is fallacious when it presents itself as giving a reason for a conclusion, or as a fair move in a discussion, but on inspection it either (i) does not give the support it claims (invalid form, irrelevance, ambiguity, or an unanswered critical question), or (ii) breaks a rule both sides would need to accept to settle the question on its merits. It is *dangerous* when it looks good enough to pass unnoticed.**

To operationalise this:
1. **Name the claimed support.** Is the argument deductive, inductive, abductive, a scheme such as authority or analogy, or a procedural move?
2. **Test that support by its own standard.** Deductive arguments are tested for validity, inductive ones for sample and base rates, schemes by their critical questions, and procedural moves by the pragma-dialectical rules.
3. **Check the words.** Do the key terms keep one meaning (Spinoza, TIE §§88–90)?
4. **Report the gap rather than only the label.** Say "this appeal to authority doesn't establish that the source is an expert in the relevant field", not just "ad verecundiam".
5. **Don't infer that the conclusion is false.** The fallacy fallacy applies to the auditor too.

---

## 9. The three most important points

1. **A fallacy is a bad move that looks good, not just a false claim or a weak argument.** The appearance condition runs from Aristotle's "apparent refutations" to Walton's "semblance of correctness". Pragma-dialectics replaces it with "any violation of the rules of critical discussion". Either way a fallacy is a *kind* of defect, not a disagreement about the facts.
2. **Context decides.** Most named informal fallacies are legitimate defeasible arguments that become fallacious only when their critical questions go unanswered or when they are used to block discussion. This is the lesson of Walton's argumentation schemes, Hahn & Oaksford's Bayesian analysis, and the Fallacy Fork. A pattern-matcher without context will mostly produce false alarms.
3. **Keep the categories apart.** Formal fallacies (invalid forms), informal fallacies (defective content or context), stratagems (unfair conduct) and biases (psychological causes of error) are different things, checked in different ways. Calling something a fallacy *starts* a critique rather than ending it, and showing an argument is bad does not show its conclusion is false.
